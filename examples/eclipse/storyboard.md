# Solar Eclipse — Guided Explainer Storyboard

Learning goal:

> After watching this, the viewer should understand that a solar eclipse needs new moon, proximity to an orbital node, near-alignment of Sun–Moon–Earth, and a shadow that actually reaches Earth.

The visual tilt in the demo is intentionally exaggerated for readability. The real lunar orbital inclination to the ecliptic is about **5.1°**.

## Step 1 — Establish the system · 0–5s

**Narration**

The Sun is the light source, Earth receives that light, and the Moon orbits Earth. A solar eclipse can only begin when the Moon comes between the Sun and Earth.

**Visible change**

- reveal Sun, Earth, Moon
- reveal the lunar orbit
- slowly establish the camera and spatial scale

**Key takeaway**

A solar eclipse is only possible near new moon, but new moon alone is not enough.

## Step 2 — Reveal orbital tilt · 5–10s

**Narration**

The lunar orbit is tilted by about 5.1° to the ecliptic. Most new moons pass above or below the Sun–Earth line, so the Moon's shadow misses Earth.

**Visible change**

- reveal the ecliptic reference plane
- reveal the tilted lunar orbital plane
- highlight the tilt callout

**Key takeaway**

Orbital tilt is why a solar eclipse does not happen every month.

## Step 3 — Approach an orbital node · 10–15s

**Narration**

The lunar orbit crosses the ecliptic at two orbital nodes. When new moon happens close to a node, the Sun, Moon and Earth can become nearly aligned.

**Visible change**

- reveal both nodes
- reveal the nodal line
- move the Moon toward the Sun–Earth line
- illuminate the alignment guide

**Key takeaway**

New moon + near a node creates an eclipse window.

## Step 4 — Build the shadow · 15–20s

**Narration**

The Moon blocks sunlight and creates a narrow umbra plus a wider penumbra behind it.

**Visible change**

- reveal umbra cone
- reveal penumbra cone
- dim the scene slightly
- label both shadow regions

**Key takeaway**

Umbra produces totality; penumbra produces a partial eclipse.

## Step 5 — Put observers on Earth · 20–25s

**Narration**

Observers inside the umbra see a total solar eclipse. Observers inside the penumbra see a partial eclipse. The moving umbra draws the narrow path of totality across Earth.

**Visible change**

- move the camera toward Earth
- mark a total-eclipse observer
- mark a partial-eclipse observer
- reveal a soft shadow spot on Earth

**Key takeaway**

The same eclipse looks different depending on where you stand.

## Step 6 — Explain why eclipses are rare · 25–30s

**Narration**

New moon, proximity to a node, and near-perfect alignment must happen at the same time. When the node line is not aimed toward the Sun, the lunar shadow passes above or below Earth.

**Visible change**

- rotate the nodal direction away from the Sun–Earth line
- move the Moon and its shadow away from Earth
- re-emphasize the two orbital planes and nodes

**Key takeaway**

A solar eclipse is a coincidence of several geometric conditions, not something that happens every lunar orbit.
